Social media give researchers vast archives of people talking among themselves, and the temptation to mine them by machine. We explain how to study online cultures as an ethnographer would, with the researcher's own participation and judgment at the centre of the analysis.

In short

The question. Over a billion people use social media to communicate and share opinions. Their exchanges are rich material for studying culture, but they are voluminous, often anonymous and hard to classify. Researchers need procedures for analyzing them rigorously.

What we did. In this handbook chapter, we set out the procedures of netnography, ethnography adapted to online communities and social media, from choosing a site to judging the quality of the analysis, and illustrate them with a short post from an online discussion.

What we found. We give four principles for analysis and show how coding and interpretation work together. Software can find and organize data, but insight comes from the researcher's immersion in the community.

What makes online data different

Online data differ from face-to-face data in four ways. Anyone with an Internet connection can reach communities whose members live far apart. People communicate through many channels at once, in public and in private, in real time or not, in text, images and sound. These channels are linked: an entry can be posted on a blog, shared on social networks, discussed on forums, mocked in a video and end up on the national news, so a single post often cannot tell the whole story. And the Internet saves most of this automatically, which is like having recordings of every public conversation in a community.

These features bring challenges. Most social worlds span online and offline life, so a study limited to online data suits only phenomena that happen online, such as online word of mouth. The volume of data can overwhelm researchers, anonymity removes demographic markers, and the mix of public and private raises questions of privacy and consent.

From entering a community to analyzing it

Researchers should choose sites that are relevant to their question and rich in posts, posters and interaction. Reading without posting is useful reconnaissance, but only as a first step: researchers should then take part, ideally contributing something useful to the community. They work with three kinds of data. Archival data exist independently of the researcher. Elicited data are created with members, for example through interviews by email, chat or video call. Field notes, kept from the start of the project, record the researcher's path from outsider to member.

Ethical practice rests on informed consent and on weighing risks to people and communities. A public post is technically published, but quoting it in research can follow its author every time someone searches the author's alias. When harm is likely, researchers should treat online pseudonyms as they would legal names and disguise them.

Coding labels data with members' own terms and groups these labels into broader categories; interpretation reads each part in light of the whole. We illustrate with a 64-word post from a LinkedIn group on netnography, found through a social media search engine. Its author asked whether anyone did small-scale, in-depth netnography of naturally formed communities, since the method was getting hard to distinguish from social media listening. Coding picked out research terms, methodological concerns and distinctions; interpretation read the post as an effort to set communities that formed on their own apart from those cultivated by brands, and to present in-depth study of them as more authentic research.

Keeping the researcher at the centre

Software for web mining and qualitative coding can automate much of this work, and it risks reducing the researcher to a button-pusher who accepts the program's output as the interpretation. Archiving whole websites can likewise crowd out engagement with the community. To keep data manageable, we relay Kozinets' guideline of no more than 1,000 double-spaced pages for analysis by hand, or 5,000 with qualitative analysis software. A smaller data set still has to be sampled rigorously, and never chosen to confirm the researcher's hunches. We also list the ten criteria Kozinets proposed for judging a netnography, among them that the analysis is coherent, linked to the data, open to alternative interpretations, and attentive to how online and offline interaction connect.

These procedures serve qualitative researchers in any discipline who study life online. Online anonymity is also declining: social networks increasingly require real names, and platforms such as Facebook in some sense own their users' data under restrictive terms of use, so researchers must check what they may collect and publish.
