Tesla drew more attention than most car brands while the market for electric cars was still forming. We show how a new brand in a new market can gain attention fast by breaking with the practices its industry accepts, and how its category gains attention with it.

In short

The question. Research on brands has mostly studied established brands with large shares of well-defined markets. We asked what makes a new brand in a new market rapidly gain wide attention from investors, consumers and the media.

What we did. We studied Tesla, an electric car brand that shipped its first car in 2008, through 1,484 pages of archival data covering the electric car category from 2000 to 2016 and the Tesla brand from 2005 to 2016.

What we found. We call the process brand celebrification: a new brand rapidly gains wide attention in a market that is still forming, and the brand and its category become visible at an accelerated pace. Tesla's celebrification came from three strategies that broke with accepted practice, combined with a largely positive response from the people and organizations watching the brand.

Legitimacy and attention in a new market

Research on new markets explains how brands gain legitimacy: a brand earns acceptance by conforming to the rules, norms and shared understandings of its market, which Scott (1995) calls the three pillars of legitimacy. This research has not examined what happens when a brand ignores the established ways of acting in its market. We combined it with research on celebrity from media sociology. Firms can become celebrities, and part of their fame comes from audiences responding well when they act against accepted practice (Rindova et al. 2006).

The case of Tesla

Tesla has been a focal point in the emergence of the electric car market, and it has generated more attention than most brands in the car industry. We collected articles from technology (Wired), green technology (Clean Technica), automotive (Motor Trend, Car and Driver), business (Wall Street Journal) and general publications (New York Times, Los Angeles Times). We added posts from the automotive blog Jalopnik and consumers' reactions to them, discussions in the investor community Seeking Alpha, and the Tesla Motors Forums.

Three strategies that broke with accepted practice

Tesla resisted categorization, defied normative expectations and over-promised. Each strategy runs against the advice that a brand gains legitimacy by conforming to its market. Investors, consumers and the media nonetheless responded mostly positively. This combination of deviance and positive response drove Tesla's celebrification and built interest in electric cars as a category.

In a market that is still forming, particularly one that has yet to grow strongly, as was the case for electric cars at the time, a brand can benefit from deviant activities as long as it convinces its stakeholders that these activities create value.

What this means for managers

A brand entering a market that is still forming does not always have to follow the norms of its wider industry to succeed commercially.

  1. Consider deviance as a strategy. Resisting categorization, defying normative expectations and over-promising drew attention to Tesla and to electric cars.
  2. Convince stakeholders of the value. The benefit depends on persuading investors, consumers and the media that the brand's deviant activities create value.
