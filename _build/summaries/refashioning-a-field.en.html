Fashion enthusiasts who post outfits, blogs and street photographs online rarely set out to change the fashion industry. We show that their everyday posting changed who does the industry's work, which players it recognizes and what it values.

In short

The question. Research on market change has focused on marketers and on unhappy consumers who try to change a market. We asked what happens to a market when contented enthusiasts, with no such agenda, connect online to share their tastes and opinions.

What we did. We studied fashion from 2011 to 2014, through participation in an outfit-sharing website and five forums, interviews, press coverage and fashion blogs.

What we found. Consumers took on part of the work of stylists, photographers and editors, and added new forms of that work. Fashion bloggers and street photographers emerged as new kinds of players, and established players contested their standing. Consumers also spread a new set of values for the field, which we call the logic of accessibility: fashion should be affordable and wearable.

How fashion enthusiasts moved online

Fashion magazines began moving online in 1994. Fashion forums such as StyleForum followed in 2002, then personal style blogs, street photography websites and, by 2008, outfit-sharing websites such as lookbook.nu, which had 1.2 million active members in 2013. Most bloggers and street photographers were unpaid consumers.

One of us spent three years posting outfits and forum messages on lookbook.nu and five forums, where 307 fans came to follow his looks. We archived 135 forum threads, interviewed 19 bloggers, outfit sharers, street photographers, buyers and designers, and analyzed 158 newspaper and trade press articles, 66 published interviews with industry actors and 4,690 pages of blog posts and comments.

What changed in the field

Consumers share and extend the industry's work. On lookbook.nu, users compose a look and photograph it, much as stylists and photographers do for a magazine, and other users draw inspiration and discover brands from these images. Consumers also added work that professionals had rarely done. Street photographers began photographing fashion editors and the guests at events such as Fashion Week, which turned editors into public figures. One street photographer told us that editors' role has changed due to the invention of our role.

New players emerge, and established players push back. Fashion bloggers developed practices of their own, such as posts about a shopping trip and a recognizable pose, and the group gained associations, conferences and a status order based on audience size. When Dolce & Gabbana seated bloggers and street photographers in its front row in 2009, it gave them laptops on little podiums, which set them apart from the established guests. Unlike music distributors, who could take file sharers to court, fashion insiders had no legal case against bloggers, so they questioned bloggers' knowledge: an Elle creative director called them "couch critics." Even prominent bloggers wrote of doubting their own legitimacy.

A new value takes hold. Consumers accepted the field's existing values of profit and artistic distinction; participants rarely questioned bloggers' sponsorships. Yet many popular posts showed looks others could afford and wear every day. A blogger with more than 45,000 fans said his style appealed because he avoided clothes "people can't get their hands on" and shopped at thrift stores. The value these posts express gives wearability more weight against artistry and separates a garment's worth from its price. Fast-fashion brands helped start this value, and consumers sped its spread without trying to displace the older ones.

Why it matters beyond fashion

Studies of market change have described consumers who agitate for it, such as music file sharers. Our case shows that contented consumers can change a market at least as widely: they redistributed marketing work, created new players, added sales channels such as Nasty Gal, which grew from selling vintage on eBay in 2007 to a $100 million online retailer by 2012, and spread a new value. Comparing our case with teenagers' invention of text messaging, we propose that such unintended change may depend on four conditions: places where consumers interact, low costs to experiment, behaviour that others can observe, and existing products and technologies to build on.

What this means for managers

In markets where enthusiasts gather online, consumers do part of the work that firms once paid professionals to do.

  1. Follow the consumers who style and photograph your products. Other consumers use their posts for inspiration and to discover brands.
  2. Expect wearability and price to count alongside prestige. Popular outfit sharers mix luxury labels with fast fashion and thrift finds.
  3. Check your market for the four conditions. Where consumers have places to meet, cheap ways to experiment, visible results and tools to build on, their uncoordinated practices can change the market.
