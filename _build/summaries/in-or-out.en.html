New tastes in fashion are usually traced to designers, magazines and other authorities. We show how members of an online forum, with little input from such authorities, built a new taste in avant-garde fashion, later known as goth ninja, by debating each other's outfits.

In short

The question. Research explains how firms create new tastes, and how social groups or classes develop tastes of their own to oppose the mainstream or to set themselves apart. It says less about the everyday exchanges through which a group of consumers settles on what counts as tasteful.

What we did. We followed the online forum stylezeitgeist from its founding in 2006 to 2012, through 35 months of participant observation, an archive of 172,242 posts and 12 interviews.

What we found. Forum members built a taste regime: shared rules about which brands and garments, ways of wearing them and meanings belong together. They did so through three processes that operate together: ruling elements out, bringing new ones in, and stabilizing the links between them.

From scattered outfits to a uniform

A consumer founded stylezeitgeist in 2006 to discuss clothes and designers instead of models and celebrities. Its front page asked that discussions divorce fashion from consumerism and celebrity culture, and it drew people who valued the uniqueness and craft they saw in avant-garde fashion. By 2012 it had 12,000 registered members and about 38,000 unique visitors a month.

These shared values did not yet add up to a look. Outfits posted in 2006 mixed colours, fits, styles and brands. By 2012, they shared a dark palette, layered tops and trousers tucked into oversized boots, worn with brands such as Rick Owens and Ann Demeulemeester. Between 2006 and 2008, eight designers traded places among the forum's most mentioned; later, four brands held the list. Members had assembled this look from designers whose aesthetics were at best loosely aligned, from Rick Owens, who shows in Paris, to Guidi, a century-old Italian tannery known for simple leather designs. By 2009, TIME referred to the look as goth ninja, and retailers and Tumblr accounts were built around it.

One of us took part in the forum for 35 months. We analyzed 172,242 posts by 3,200 members, focusing on 74 threads with lively debate about aesthetics, counted words over time to trace the rise and decline of brands, practices and meanings, and interviewed 12 members aged 19 to 33.

How the forum built the taste

Ruling out. Members pushed brands and ways of dressing out by tying them to meanings the forum disparaged. Raf Simons was among the most admired brands from 2006 to 2008, then declined sharply from 2010. Members opened a thread on what they saw as his failures; one called a printed beach towel a sellout made for money. Members also debated how to tuck trousers into boots, and dismissed one heavy-soled pair as looking "a little too construction worker."

Bringing in. Members added new items when these carried meanings the forum valued. A member posted an outfit with a hat by Number (N)ine, a designer the forum had largely overlooked. The hat expressed the forum's taste for somberness, and the post drew a stream of compliments; another member then posted an outfit with a similar hat as a very literal nod to the first. Members with broad fashion knowledge did this most successfully. One posted step-by-step instructions for restyling a Junya Watanabe skirt that another member had found hard to wear.

Stabilizing. In threads such as "What Are You Wearing Today," members praised outfits that combined approved items in approved ways, and these posts made the rules visible to everyone. Members also defended approved brands. When one member said the Carpe Diem designers made sportswear without real craft, another compared Carpe Diem's designer Amadei with Carol Christian Poell, a designer the forum admired, to restore the brand's link to craftsmanship. One member, after strangers asked whether the outfit was religious dress, described it to the forum as "the uniform."

Why it matters beyond fashion

Without authorities to set standards, one might expect avant-garde consumers, who prize innovation and uniqueness, to multiply their styles. These consumers instead patched together a new and increasingly strict taste from existing brands and garments. We predict that early on, ruling out and bringing in set the core items, and changes in taste are often drastic; once a taste settles, stabilizing matters more and change becomes more incremental. Groups like this one change what is considered tasteful without changing the kinds of players in a market or the values that guide it. We expect such groups to flourish where consumers have places to interact, cheap ways to experiment and visible practices, and to shape wider tastes when competent members engage with less competent ones.
