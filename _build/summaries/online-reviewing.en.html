Millions of people write online reviews, and they write them in very different ways. We show that these differences come from what reviewers believe a review is for, and that each belief produces a recognizable way of writing.

In short

The question. Why do people review the same hotel, restaurant or book so differently? Research has looked for the answer in reviewers' personalities, motives, demographics and expertise, and has assumed that everyone shares the same idea of what a review is.

What we did. We analyzed 388 TripAdvisor reviews of three French tourist sites, 480 Amazon reviews written by 96 reviewers, and interviews with 50 reviewers alongside 507 reviews they had published.

What we found. Reviewers draw on different shared understandings of what reviewing is, which we call reviewing orientations. We identify three. Each produces a distinct voice that stays consistent across a reviewer's reviews and appears on every platform we studied.

Three ways to understand a review

Communal sharers see themselves as ordinary consumers in a community of equals, and they write for other consumers. They review when an experience stands out, good or bad, and they tell its story in the first person and the past tense, emotions included. A nutritionist reviewing a pizzeria wrote about her pleasure in the meal, without mentioning nutrition. For them, the pool of reviews is a commons; one interviewee described TripAdvisor as a large corkboard where everyone can put a thumbtack.

Systemic evaluators see themselves as one contributor among millions in a system of platforms, businesses and consumers that improves as reviews accumulate. They review regularly, out of a sense of duty, and they keep themselves out of the text. Their reviews read like short reports on features, in the present tense and an even tone: "Very nice brunch, homemade dishes. Well-portioned quantities, for a standard price (€23)."

Competitive pundits see reviewing as a contest for attention. They present themselves as experts and guides, write catchy titles, track their badges and levels, and rarely read other people's reviews. Their reviews instruct the reader: "If you go to Burzet, go to La Framboise!" They review to stand out, to correct other reviewers or guidebooks, or to reveal a place they think deserves to be known.

Why it matters beyond reviews

Research on reviews has asked what makes a review helpful and has produced mixed answers. Our findings suggest one reason: helpfulness means different things to different reviewers. A communal sharer finds an honest story helpful, while a systemic evaluator finds a fair list of strengths and weaknesses helpful. Studies that assume reviewers compete for attention also describe only one of the three groups, the competitive pundits. And storytelling and persuasion, often treated together, belong to different orientations: communal sharers tell stories, while pundits persuade by instructing.

The same lens applies to any activity people perform in different ways, such as peer review or restaurant criticism. In each, it asks what people believe the activity is for before asking how individuals differ.

What this means for managers

Review platforms and brands deal with three kinds of reviewer, and each responds to different design and different replies.

  1. Design for the reviewers you want. Profiles and friend networks invite communal sharers, as on Letterboxd. Anonymous reviews, prompts for specific criteria and a request after each purchase invite systemic evaluators, as on Shein. Tiers, badges and free products invite pundits, as on Influenster.
  2. Reply to each group in its own terms. Respond personally and with empathy to communal sharers. Answer systemic evaluators with facts and the steps you are taking to fix a problem. Recognize pundits' expertise, and take disputes with them offline.
  3. Read each group for different insight. Communal sharers reveal the emotional side of an experience, systemic evaluators its strengths and weaknesses across features, and pundits emerging trends and unmet needs.
