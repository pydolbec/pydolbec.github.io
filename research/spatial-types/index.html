Conceptualizing Spatial Types: Characteristics, Transitions, and Research Avenues

Rodrigo Castilhos and Pierre-Yann Dolbec

Streets, shopping malls, gated communities and festivals each shape how people consume, and cities keep turning one kind of space into another. We sort urban spaces into four types and explain how public, private and protest spaces become commercial ones.

Four types of space, set apart by two oppositions

Public space belongs to the public and is governed by public authority: streets, squares, parks and beaches. Anyone legally allowed to take part in society may use it, but that does not make it democratic or egalitarian. Groups negotiate its use with one another through taste, etiquette and behaviour, and they contest uses they see as unfit, as when local authorities restrict skateboarding.

Market space is owned and governed by firms: shops, shopping centres and entertainment venues. It admits those who can afford it or who have the cultural capital to behave as expected, and its design assigns consumers the roles they are to play. Consumers there negotiate with the firm's material and symbolic narrative, where in public space they negotiate with each other.

Segregating space serves a cohesive group: homes, residential enclaves and private clubs, and also ghettos and favelas. Membership rests on social affiliation, and residents police the rules themselves. Consensus in these spaces reproduces the social order.

Emancipating space is created by a community to challenge a dominant order: festivals such as Burning Man, carnivals, or a public square that a movement takes over for a time. People enter by joining voluntarily and sharing the community's ideology. Consensus in these spaces subverts the social order, and the spaces are usually temporary.

Where research on each type stands

Market spaces are the most studied in marketing, through work on servicescapes and flagship stores. Segregating spaces have received little attention apart from the home, although condominiums and gated communities increasingly shape consumers' social environments. Each type leaves questions open, such as how consumers from different social classes negotiate a public space, how firms use the design and discourse of a space to segment consumers in ways that some groups read as exclusion, and how flash mobs create brief emancipating spaces in everyday city life. The typology also shows who produces each type. Marketers are central to market spaces, regulators act as gatekeepers of public spaces, and consumers define the consensus on which segregating and emancipating spaces rest.

How public space becomes market space

The four types change as forces in society change. Service economies grow, cities compete for outside investment, and investors put unprecedented sums into urban land and property. The state and market actors such as developers, architects, investors and realtors align in public–private partnerships that favour particular projects, such as museum quarters and office parks. Three transitions follow, all toward market space.

Boston's waterfront shows the move from public to market space. Rezoned since the mid-1960s from industrial toward mixed use, it was renovated with an estimated US$40 billion of public and private investment. Its design favours middle-class leisure, such as picnics, walking and watching the water, and restricts the "noisy" leisure of the working class. The renovation favoured hotels, offices, luxury housing, upscale shops, marinas, museums, bars and restaurants, and the waterfront's brand invites visitors to eat, stay, play, shop, work! Such transitions are usually preceded by talk of public areas as decadent, empty and dangerous, and they displace groups who cannot compete under the logic of the market.

Because this shift favours some groups over others, it creates value for some actors and destroys it for others. Studying it can show how actors use space to build legitimacy and silence opposing groups, and what the shift costs vulnerable groups. Marketers are among its main actors, and research has yet to explain the tools they use in it.

In short

The question. Marketing scholars have studied many kinds of space, such as homes, shopping malls, neighbourhoods and cities. No one had worked out how these spaces relate to one another, what sets them apart, and how their differences shape what consumers experience in them.

What we did. In this conceptual essay, we draw on work on how societies produce space to build a typology of urban space. For each type, we map existing research in marketing and the questions it leaves open.

What we found. Cities contain four types of space: public, market, segregating and emancipating. Two oppositions separate them: whether people negotiate a space's uses or share a consensus about them, and whether the space invites participation or aims to produce particular kinds of people. As the state, developers and investors align in public–private partnerships, a confluence of forces we call the nexus of marketization, other types of space turn into market space.
