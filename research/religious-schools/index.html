How Established Organizations Combine Logics to Reconfigure Resources and Adapt to Marketization: A Case Study of Brazilian Religious Schools

Pierre-Yann Dolbec, Rodrigo Castilhos, Marcelo J. Fonseca, and Guilherme Trez

When markets reach fields such as education, health care or the arts, the organizations that were there first must compete without giving up what they stand for. We show how five Brazilian Jesuit schools did both, by adapting their offer to the market while keeping their religious mission at its centre.

Elite schools facing a market

For most of the last century, these Catholic schools educated Brazil's elite. Their reputation rested on tradition, spirituality and a holistic education that trained students in mind, body and spirit. Neoliberal reforms in the late 1980s and early 1990s opened private education to for-profit schools, which competed on national test rankings, international curricula and employability. Parents began to ask about rankings at enrolment meetings, and competitors advertised heavily and offered scholarships to the Jesuit schools' best students. As one academic director put it, people think that what is traditional is retrograde, outdated.

The schools could not simply copy their competitors. Training students only to pass standardized tests contradicted their educational philosophy, and managers at first refused to respond to a market that, in one priest's words, was "the voice of capitalism." By the early 2010s their position had worsened, and in 2014 the Jesuit order created the network to help them adapt.

Four ways to absorb market demands

We distinguish the core of a school, such as its educational philosophy and teaching methods, from its periphery, such as extracurricular activities. Changes to the core risk internal conflict; changes at the periphery leave more room to act.

In the core, the schools kept the religious mission in charge. Instead of chasing rankings, they rewrote holistic education to give cognitive, socioemotional and spiritual learning equal weight, and translated it into learning maps that teachers used to score and revise their courses. Demands that fit the mission were blended in. Students' responsibility for their own learning echoed the Jesuit ideal of autonomy, so the schools trained teachers in flipped classrooms and project-based learning. A Portuguese teacher had fifth-graders prepare and run a debate on bullying.

At the periphery, the schools met market demands in full but kept them apart from the core. Outside providers ran practice national exams on weekends, and at one school a specialized company taught robotics. The schools also used the periphery for religion, partly because the reworked core had reduced Bible teaching. They added weekend spiritual retreats and expanded catechesis into a five-year program of initiation into Christian life.

Why it matters beyond schools

Research on organizations that combine conflicting values has focused on how they conform to each set and manage the resulting tensions. The schools show that combining them can also set an organization apart from competitors. They also combined values demand by demand, keeping some separate and blending others, instead of separating or merging whole systems of values. Museums and hospitals face similar pressures as they court visitors and treat patients as customers, and this approach offers them a way to compete without abandoning their mission.

In short

The question. In many fields, consumer choice and competition have displaced older sources of authority. Established organizations then face two problems: they lose legitimacy, and the market's demands often contradict the values on which they were built. How can they adapt?

What we did. From 2017 to 2020, we studied five schools of the Brazilian Jesuit Educational Network, founded between 1890 and 1957. We interviewed 135 experts, managers, teachers and parents, read internal documents, and observed strategy meetings.

What we found. The schools placed market demands inside their religious mission, a strategy we call nested coupling. How they handled each demand depended on whether it fit their mission and whether it touched the core of the school or its periphery.

What this means for managers

Leaders of established organizations in fields such as education, health care or culture can adapt to market pressure without abandoning their mission.

  1. Sort market demands by fit. Blend demands that fit your mission into your core offer, and fold those that clash into your mission's own terms.
  2. Use the periphery for full responses. Meet demands your core cannot absorb through optional activities, run with partners and kept apart from your core offer.
  3. Use the periphery to protect your identity. Where changes to the core dilute your mission, strengthen it through activities that draw on assets competitors lack, as the schools did with their clergy and churches.
