Between Sharing and Hiding: How Consumers Shade Knowledge

Aron Darmody, Pierre-Yann Dolbec, Mujde Yuksel, and Meera Venkatraman

Fishers asked where they caught a big fish often answer in part: they name the lake and keep the spot to themselves. We show why consumers share only part of what they know, and the six ways they do it.

Four reasons to tell or to keep quiet

Fishing depends on knowledge of spots, depths, baits and timing, while fish and good spots are finite. What fishers tell depends on whether telling would help or harm two things: their own future fishing, and the future of fishing as a shared activity. This gives four clear cases. Fishers hide a prime spot because others would crowd it; as Caleb put it, There's no reward for me sharing a spot with the world. They share techniques that show their skill and bring tips in return. They hide some knowledge to protect the activity, for example after watching others take more than their limit at a revealed spot, or to uphold the belief that newcomers should earn their spots. And they share what keeps fishing going for everyone, such as proper catch-and-release technique.

Six ways to tell part of what you know

Fishers share part of what they know when two of these cases point in opposite directions. When the YouTuber Mike Borger posted a trip with his son that produced large brook trout in Algonquin Park, he did not name the lake, and someone filed a freedom-of-information request to find it. Arlo had caught some of the season's first steelhead trout, news that sends crowds to the river. When a fishing-shop employee asked what he was catching, he said rainbow trout, and when asked where, he said "upriver," without mentioning that his spot lay 10 to 15 kilometres up. He wanted the shop to do well, but not at the expense of his own enjoyment.

Greg and his friends call one of their lakes "Tall Pines," a name they made up, so that anyone who overhears them looks for a lake that does not exist. Sally, who worked in a fly shop, gave young women starting out alone far more help than other customers, such as where to find cell service and which crowded camp areas to avoid. Max trades knowledge, usually in person: If you're telling me a good spot, I'll give you a good spot.

Why it matters beyond fishing

Research has mostly linked sharing with helping a community and hiding with self-interest. Fishers show that hiding can also protect the community, by keeping fragile places from being overfished and by preserving the norm that newcomers learn through their own effort, while sharing can deplete scarce resources. Sharing in part lets fishers keep contributing while protecting what they value, and over time these choices can reinforce an activity's norms or set new precedents for how knowledge circulates.

Fishing is competitive and its resources are scarce. In activities such as gardening, sharing may add to resources instead of depleting them, and consumers may relate to knowledge differently.

In short

The question. Research has treated consumers as either sharing what they know, which helps a community, or hiding it to keep an edge. Yet people often share and hide at once, for reasons we knew little about.

What we did. We interviewed 47 lake fishers in the United States and Canada, most of them around the Great Lakes, and read every thread posted on two Reddit fishing forums over two months each.

What we found. We call partial sharing knowledge shading. They do so when telling would serve one of their goals and keeping quiet would serve another. They do it in six ways, such as giving a code name to a lake or trading a spot only for another one.

What this means for managers

Brands that host consumer communities, such as forums and apps, can support partial sharing or limit it.

  1. Give members control over what they reveal. Let them schedule how long a post stays visible, blur details in photos and track how widely a post circulates. To limit it, require location and timing data on photos and videos.
  2. Build sub-groups and mentoring. Let members exchange knowledge in sub-groups of similar experience, offer mentorship, and rotate moderators so that knowledge and power do not concentrate.
  3. Reward careful sharing. Award badges to members who balance contribution with protection, and unlock scarce knowledge once members have earned enough participation points.
