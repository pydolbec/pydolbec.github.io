The Fallacy of Facility: Concerns Regarding the (Mis)Use of GGT

Pierre-Yann Dolbec, Eileen Fischer, and Andrew Smith

Generative AI makes it easy to produce what looks like qualitative analysis. We argue that researchers can mistake this ease for expertise, and we set out what they need in order to use AI in qualitative research without losing rigour.

How ease passes for expertise

We see AI as a useful tool for qualitative research. Directing AI to carry out research tasks can help researchers from diverse backgrounds, for example to sketch possible research routes or to run light initial analyses. However, studies show that people delegate cognitive tasks to AI, submit its outputs with little or no revision, and sometimes adopt outputs that are wrong. A researcher who moves through the literature review, analysis and writing by prompting and accepting plausible outputs may take those outputs for scholarly understanding. This weakens the evidentiary, methodological and interpretive accountability on which academic knowledge depends. The risk is greatest for researchers who have not yet learned to analyze qualitative data without AI.

Three risks, shown in the lead article's examples

First, researchers can defer to AI, granting it undue authority because its output sounds confident. In the lead article, the researchers generated nine additional synthetic cases, modelled as digital twins, and asked the model whether the proposed pathway was confirmed. The model reported that the pathway appeared unambiguously across 12 cases, with the same process in the same order and the same four elements. That uniformity should be a warning that the model may be confirming what it was asked to confirm. The model gave no case-by-case analysis, excerpts, negative cases or audit, so its answer cannot be evaluated.

Second, AI can strip data of the situations and researcher involvement that make it interpretable. The lead article coded 15 synthetic interviews alongside forum data, although digital twins are ideally built from rich data on specific people and here no real person stood behind them. It also processed posts from Reddit and Trustpilot as free-floating text, cut off from the platform norms, conversations and community relations that give them meaning. An analysis built this way can appear empirically rich while resting on data that does not support it.

Third, AI can perform something that carries a method's name without its substance. In the lead article, a single prompt asked the model for open codes and returned more than 100 labels, many of them interpretations such as reclaiming body with no tie to specific excerpts. Open coding in grounded theory works through comparison, memo writing and grounding each code in the data, and the article shows no evidence that these steps took place.

What researchers need to use AI well

We propose three commitments. The first is proficiency with AI: knowing what AI systems can and cannot do, how they can hallucinate, smooth over ambiguity or misread context, and documenting prompts and outputs so that others can inspect the role AI played. The second is keeping data in context. Interviews, field notes, archival material, online discourse, synthetic outputs and AI-generated summaries are different kinds of evidence, so researchers must specify what each can support and keep primary data central. The third is methodological expertise, applied before AI. Researchers engage with the data themselves, through manual coding, close reading, memo writing, comparison and negative case analysis, and they check AI-generated codes and themes against the data and the method's standards. Without such expertise, a researcher cannot tell rigorous AI-assisted analysis from a simulation of the method.

In short

The question. Schmitt et al. (2026) proposed Generative Grounded Theory, a method that uses generative AI to build theory from qualitative data with greater scale and efficiency. In a commentary on their article, we asked what can go wrong when researchers use such a method.

What we did. We examined the examples in their article against the procedures of grounded theory and the standards of qualitative research.

What we found. By making information easy to produce, organize and access, AI can make users feel competent. We call this the fallacy of facility: the tendency to mistake ease for expertise, which creates unwarranted confidence in one's understanding of complex phenomena. Research susceptible to it faces three related risks, and countering them requires expertise in both AI and qualitative methods.
