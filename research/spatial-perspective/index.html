Journal article · Marketing Theory, 2017

Introducing a Spatial Perspective to Analyze Market Dynamics

Rodrigo Castilhos, Pierre-Yann Dolbec, and Ela Veresiu

Seattle's coffee culture helped create Starbucks, and Silicon Valley's mix of entrepreneurs, universities and investors helped make it the centre of the technology industry. We give marketing scholars four ways to analyze space and identify twelve ways in which space shapes markets.

Four ways to look at space

Geographers treat space as the ongoing product of relations among people, things and institutions. A place is a bounded space that people invest with meaning and value, from a favourite armchair to the whole earth. A territory is a bounded space where individuals, groups or institutions control access, purposes and meanings, so analyzing territory brings power, borders and control to the fore. Scale orders the world in levels, such as local, national and global, and draws attention to vertical relations between them. A network links dispersed entities horizontally and draws attention to flows among them. We put place at the centre, because place is the ingredient that structures territories, scales and networks.

Twelve ways space shapes markets

Places give market meanings physical form, frame what people experience, and bond people to one another and to places. At a rodeo show, breed shows, auctions and exhibitors' booths gave form to the meanings the beef industry wanted attached to cattle, and helped legitimize the industry. Early modern Ottoman coffeehouses let people of different classes meet and relax, which contributed to an Ottoman public sphere and to the formation of the consumer subject. Botox parties held in consumers' homes made the procedure feel safe to try and helped spread the product.

Territories protect fringe groups and practices, empower the people inside them, and constrain how those people behave. Burning Man's organizers and participants drew boundaries that removed the festival from the market. In Turkish hair salons, underclass men gained cultural, social and economic capital that strengthened their standing with wealthier clients. In racially segregated neighbourhoods, residents felt a moral obligation to support local businesses. Studies of this constraint have focused on vulnerable groups, so its effect on affluent groups remains to be studied.

Scales impose market elements from larger levels on smaller ones, smaller levels resist larger ones, and actors negotiate the tensions between levels. Turkey's national ideology of westernized, secular life structured city markets and was imposed on second-generation migrants in a squatter neighbourhood outside Ankara. When national industry co-opted the organic food movement, local actors created community-supported agriculture. Most often, actors at different levels negotiate: the presence of Latino consumers in the United States, and the marketing aimed at them, changed the US market.

Networks carry market elements across places, combine elements in ways that change what each can do, and strengthen or weaken a market through the links among its places. Starbucks' stores carry a shared understanding of the brand around the world. A tattoo parlour beside a gallery makes a different scene from a tattoo parlour beside a biker bar, although market research has rarely studied such combinations. Local riding communities and tracks helped turn a loose community of minimoto riders into a functioning market.

What a spatial view adds to the study of markets

The mechanisms add to existing explanations. Research has shown that new markets require recruiting actors; we add that they also require networks of places that carry market elements from one place to another. Research has shown that consumers can change markets; we add that small-scale places and the territories in which consumers live give them room to resist. We recommend combining dimensions, because analyzing one alone mistakes a part of space for the whole. Combinations open new questions, such as how global brands impose their definition of retail experience on local places, or how homes, streets and shops in a neighbourhood help produce the kind of consumer who furthers gentrification. For scholars in other fields, the paper carries the spatial turn in the social sciences, which treats space as an active participant in social life, into the study of how markets form and change.

In short

The question. Research on how markets form and change has focused on what consumers and producers do, and has treated space as a backdrop. We asked how space, in its different forms, affects markets.

What we did. We drew on geography to define four dimensions of space: place, territory, scale and network. We then reanalyzed 47 consumer research articles, retained from an initial set of 59, to see how each dimension figures in accounts of how markets are created, maintained and disrupted.

What we found. Each dimension offers a distinct vantage point, and together they form a spatial perspective on market dynamics. We identify twelve mechanisms, three for each dimension, through which space shapes markets.
