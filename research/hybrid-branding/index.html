Entering a Complex Market: How Hybrid Branding Helps New Brands Create Distinctive and Resonant Identities

Nicole Gorman and Pierre-Yann Dolbec

A new brand that adopts the meanings its competitors already use struggles to stand out. We show how Savage X Fenty, Rihanna's lingerie brand, built a distinctive identity by combining two opposing views of lingerie that already divided its market.

In short

The question. Many markets are divided between opposing sets of beliefs, values and practices, such as spiritual, fitness and medical yoga. A new brand that adopts one of them joins a crowd of brands drawing on the same meanings. How can it build an identity that stands out and resonates with consumers?

What we did. We studied the lingerie market from 2005 to 2020 and Savage X Fenty from its 2018 launch, using more than 1,500 news and magazine articles, 67 podcast episodes, the brand's social media posts and recordings of its four fashion shows.

What we found. Savage X Fenty combined elements from both sides of its market, a strategy we call hybrid branding. It did so through four mechanisms, and the combination exposed it to three kinds of criticism.

What this means for managers ↓

A market split in two

Before 2018, two views organized the lingerie market. The first, which we call the bombshell view, treated lingerie as a way for women to fulfil men's sexual fantasies. Victoria's Secret embodied it: tall, thin models, push-up bras and corsets, and a yearly runway show watched by 10 million consumers. At its peak, the brand held 33% of the market. The second, the body-positive view, emerged against the first. Brands such as Aerie showed unretouched women of many body types in comfortable bralettes and briefs, and kept sexuality out of their communication. By the late 2010s, body-positive brands were growing and Victoria's Secret's sales were falling. A newcomer joining either side would face stiff competition.

Savage X Fenty joined neither. Its identity rested on inclusive sexual empowerment: women and gender non-conforming people of any size, race or sexual orientation can be sexy on their own terms, and feel empowered by it. Two years after launch, its revenues exceeded $150 million, and it later held an estimated 5% of the market.

Four ways to combine opposing views

The brand's fashion shows, a tactic Victoria's Secret had made famous, display the four mechanisms.

The first two mechanisms place the brand relative to competitors without creating new meanings. Victoria's Secret models signalled sexuality, while footage of Rihanna in a hoodie, planning the launch, signalled the realness of body-positive brands. Bella Hadid, who had walked for Victoria's Secret, said that Rihanna's show was the first time on a runway that I felt really sexy. The last two mechanisms create meanings the market did not have. Placing Paris Hilton and Margie Plus on equal footing proposed that both bodies were desirable. Lizzo's performance, set in a mirrored room, a familiar trope of bombshell advertising, presented a plus-size body as sexy and its display as empowering.

The risks of a hybrid identity

Critics faulted the brand for departing from body-positive expectations, for example with a first size range that stopped at 3X. Some saw its sexualized shows as extending sexualization to plus-size women instead of resolving the contradiction. Others noted that its Valentine's Day collection offered more covered-up designs to plus-size women, which clashed with its promise of sexiness for all bodies. And because a hybrid brand already draws on both views, it may have no other position to move to when criticized.

Why it matters for research on brands

Research has shown that brands create resonant meanings by resolving contradictions among consumers, within a brand's history or in society, as Dove's Real Beauty campaign did. We add the market itself as a source of such contradictions. Markets for coffee, food, cars and yoga are also divided between opposing views, and brands such as Starbucks Reserve combine elements of both. Established organizations can combine views to adapt when a new one comes to dominate their market, while a new brand can build a hybrid identity from the start, through what it communicates.

What this means for managers

Brands entering a divided market can turn its contradictions into a distinctive identity.

  1. Map the views that divide your market. Collect trade press, consumer conversations and competitors' materials, and note the vocabulary, products, spokespeople and practices of each view.
  2. Choose how far to combine them. Combine both views equally, as Savage X Fenty did; add elements of a second view to a primary one, as Starbucks Reserve did with craft coffee; or heighten the tension for provocation, as Vetements did in fashion.
  3. Prepare for criticism from both sides. Critics may judge the brand by the standards of either view, read its combinations as deepening the contradiction, or fault products that fall short of its promise, as with the Valentine's Day collection.
