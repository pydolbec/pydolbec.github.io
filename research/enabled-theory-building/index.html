Something Old, Something New: Enabled Theory Building in Qualitative Marketing Research

Pierre-Yann Dolbec, Eileen Fischer, and Robin Canniford

Qualitative researchers in marketing often read their data through an existing theory, and critics say the habit produces applications of old ideas instead of new ones. We show how researchers work with these theories, and how the theories help them make original contributions.

What a borrowed theory helps researchers contribute

Most of the papers we analyzed used a theory to advance a debate that already existed. Giesler (2012) drew on early actor–network theory to show that a brand's image is always at stake, because several stakeholders use intermediaries such as mainstream media to advance competing images. A second, less common kind of contribution corrects or extends the theory itself. Thomas and Epp (2019) added the concept of envisioned practices to practice theory to explain how practices become habits, and Humphreys (2010) added a territorial dimension to institutional theory's account of legitimacy. A third kind informs policy, practice or method. Canniford and Shankar (2013) showed that consumers hide the links between nature and culture to preserve their experience of nature, which masks environmental damage, and they proposed practices that build more sustainable links.

Each theory directs attention to different features of markets and consumption. Practice theory points to shared routines and the objects, doings and meanings bundled in them. Institutional theory points to how markets form, gain acceptance and change. Assemblage perspectives point to how human and nonhuman things combine into temporary wholes that make some outcomes possible and block others. Researchers also combine theories, from one intellectual lineage or from several, when a single theory lacks the concepts their data require.

How researchers choose and change lenses

Researchers told us that a lens can shape every stage of a project without settling any of them in advance. Some bring a lens from the start; others choose one after a first round of data. Whenever researchers adopt it, the lens reshapes the project: one researcher who adopted a new lens midway had to redefine the research questions and reanalyze the data completely. For many, lenses matter most during coding and analysis, where they supply categories and labels that researchers map onto open coding without replacing it.

Some described a lens as part of how they see. A practice theorist said: Not saying everything is a practice, but everything I look at is. Others chose strategically, asking which lens would yield an insight no one had published. Such choices do not always hold. One team began with assemblage theory and switched to practice theory because, in their data, people held the agency. Some teams deliberately read the same data through several theories to see what each reveals, much like Langley's (1999) strategy of alternate templates in organization studies.

Review processes also change lenses. Every interviewee had been asked during review to add, drop or change one. They thought reviewers were sometimes right to question a lens and sometimes pushing their own pet ideas. One team changed its lens in a third round, at an editor's request, and said the change helped finish the paper. Because of how review works, published papers are rarely mere applications of a theory. Many interviewees also said that review teams expected them to use an existing theory. We see this expectation as a threat, because rejecting qualitative work that does not use one risks narrowing what our field publishes.

What counts as an original contribution

Original ideas draw on earlier ones. The critics cite brand relationship theory and brand community as original, yet earlier ideas of animism informed the first and existing theory of community the second. We propose judging originality by whether research expands horizons, and we identify four ways an existing theory helps it do so: new accounts of what a phenomenon consists of, concepts that reveal hidden aspects of consumption and markets, tools for solving practical problems, and ideas that other disciplines take up.

In short

The question. Many qualitative studies borrow an existing theory, such as practice theory or institutional theory, to make sense of their data. A recent critique argued that this habit constrains research and diminishes its originality. We asked how researchers use these theories and what the theories let them contribute.

What we did. We analyzed 30 recent papers, ten each drawing on assemblage and actor–network perspectives, institutional theory and practice theory, and interviewed ten researchers who write and review such work.

What we found. We call the practice enabled theorizing: using existing theories or conceptual lenses to inform moments of a qualitative study. It produces three kinds of contribution, and researchers combine, test and replace lenses as a project and its review unfold. We suggest that originality depends less on the theory than on how researchers combine, apply and invent with its concepts.
