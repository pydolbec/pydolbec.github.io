The Impact of a Flagship vs. a Brand Store on Brand Attitude, Brand Attachment and Brand Equity

Pierre-Yann Dolbec and Jean-Charles Chebat

A growing number of brands open flagship stores designed to be experienced as much as shopped. We show that a flagship builds a brand more than an ordinary store of the same brand, because shoppers experience the brand more intensely there.

Why compare a flagship with a regular store

A flagship carries a single brand, belongs to that brand's manufacturer and exists at least partly to reinforce the brand rather than to sell at a profit. A brand store shares the first two features. Brands now offer flagships built for experience, and studies of Niketown, ESPN Zone and American Girl Place have shown how they stage a brand's story, ideology and spectacle. Those studies did not compare a flagship with the same brand's ordinary stores, and they did not measure how much either kind of store contributes to the brand.

We asked whether store image, meaning the overall impression a shopper holds of a store, shapes three outcomes: how favourably shoppers evaluate the brand (brand attitude), how strongly they feel bound to it (brand attachment), and the value they attach to its name (brand equity). We expected brand experience to carry this effect, and the flagship to strengthen it.

A fashion brand's flagship and one of its eleven stores

We worked with a mid-tier fashion brand aimed at women aged 25 to 45. In one major North American city, it owns a flagship and eleven brand stores. The flagship occupies a three-storey downtown building and carries the whole product line, with minimalist decor, high ceilings and oversized pictures and mirrors. A museum-style display of the collection and the brand's history lines the staircase that connects the floors. A pretest confirmed that the flagship rated higher than the regular store on store image and brand experience.

Over one month, we handed questionnaires to shoppers leaving the flagship and one regular store. Of 131 respondents, 122 returned usable questionnaires, 64 at the flagship and 58 at the regular store. All were women who already knew the brand, and more than half were aged 25 to 45. We measured brand experience on four dimensions (sensory, emotional, behavioural and intellectual) and tested the relationships with structural equation modelling.

What the flagship adds

A favourable store image improved all three outcomes, and it worked through brand experience. Brand experience fully carried the effect of store image on brand attachment and brand equity, and partly carried its effect on brand attitude, which store image also shaped directly. One possible reason is that shoppers visiting several stores on a trip may face too many cues to process closely, so they may judge the brand from the store's overall impression.

Store type changed the first link. For the same store image, shoppers in the flagship reported a stronger brand experience than shoppers in the regular store. We attribute this to the flagship's many points where shoppers can sense, feel, act on and think about the brand, which turn the same favourable impression of the store into a stronger experience of the brand. Ours was the first study to measure the size of these relationships.

In short

The question. Research has described how flagships such as Niketown or American Girl Place stage a brand, but no study had measured how much a flagship builds a brand compared with a regular store of the same brand.

What we did. We surveyed 122 women as they left either the flagship or a regular store of a mid-tier fashion brand, and modelled how their image of the store related to their views of the brand.

What we found. A good store image builds the brand mainly through brand experience: the sensations, feelings, thoughts and actions that the brand evokes in the store. For the same store image, the flagship produced a stronger brand experience than the regular store.

What this means for managers

Flagships and brand stores work as promotional tools as well as sales channels, and their value runs through the experience they give shoppers.

  1. Charge for the experience through prices. Because brand experience raises the value shoppers attach to the brand name, a brand could pass the cost of an experiential store into product prices instead of charging an entrance fee.
  2. Engage all four dimensions of the experience. Appeal to shoppers' senses, emotions, actions and thoughts, for example through distinct areas within the store, as Niketown and ESPN Zone do.
  3. Fit the experience to the brand. Screens and sports memorabilia are enough for a sports bar and too little for ESPN Zone. The experience has to carry meanings that belong to the brand alone.
