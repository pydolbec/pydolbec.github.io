Reflections on Template Creep and Replicability Concerns in Contemporary Qualitative Research

Pierre-Yann Dolbec and Eileen Fischer

Qualitative research in marketing is more accepted than ever, yet some reviewers and journals increasingly ask it to follow set formulas and to prove it can be replicated. We explain why both demands can weaken the research they aim to protect, and how to secure rigour instead.

Two pressures on qualitative research

A methodological template is a standard way of conducting research that serves as a formula for collecting and analyzing data. Management researchers follow Eisenhardt's case study method or Gioia's version of grounded theory. In marketing, Humphreys' approach to large archives of media texts and the phenomenological interview have become templates, and so has the practice of using an existing theory to guide a study, which we helped define in earlier work. Templates make a difficult craft easier to learn, reassure unsure reviewers, and suit some projects well. Used as formulas without adaptation, however, they cannot fit the particulars of each project, and they can stifle novelty in both analyses and theories. As Pratt and colleagues put it, A template is at most a map, while the project is the terrain.

The second pressure comes from experimental research. After many findings proved hard to reproduce, open science practices spread, encouraging or requiring researchers to share data and materials and to preregister their designs and hypotheses. These practices assume that trustworthy research must be replicable. Most qualitative research assumes that the conditions behind any knowledge claim keep changing, so transparency about methods serves another purpose: it helps other researchers identify settings roughly similar to those where an earlier theory seemed relevant. In practice, preregistration makes no sense when a study's design deliberately emerges from ongoing analysis. Interview guides change as insights accumulate, and two interviewers using the same guide might still collect different data. Ethics boards often forbid sharing interview data, informants told that their words will be shared may decline or say less, and scrubbing data to protect them may strip away context. And because the researcher is the main instrument of analysis, another scholar with different training might draw different insights from the same data.

Rigour without a formula

We treat templates as tools, which are only as good as the person using them. Researchers should know why, how and when to use the templates of their chosen tradition, understand the philosophical assumptions each carries, develop their craft through practice, and write methods sections that explain their analytical decisions instead of repeating stock phrases such as "iterative cycling" between data and theory.

Template creep is also a problem of the field, so it requires action from experienced researchers. As reviewers, they should set aside their own theoretical and methodological preferences and meet authors on the authors' own philosophical ground. As editors, they should tell authors when reviewers' requests are optional, and sometimes ask reviewers to tone down a review. As mentors, they should teach qualitative methods, which business school doctoral programs do not teach systematically. Journals should include qualitative researchers on their editorial review boards.

For trustworthiness, we recommend criteria that match the research tradition: guidelines for positivist case studies emphasize construct, internal and external validity, while those for naturalistic inquiry emphasize credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. Reviewers should not impose their own tradition's criteria and should tell editors when a submission lies outside their expertise. Authors should state their tradition and explain why the context suited the study and how they collected and analyzed the data. Data transparency may be impossible, but methodological transparency is essential.

Why it matters beyond marketing

Management scholars have debated templates in recent special issues, and calls to make qualitative research replicable have come from management journals as well. We accept that qualitative research must be conducted and reported rigorously and transparently, and we argue that it should be judged by the criteria of its own traditions. Imitating what looks like trustworthy research in other traditions may cost qualitative research the creativity that makes it a source of novel theories and highly cited papers.

In short

The question. A growing range of premier marketing journals publish qualitative research, and these papers are disproportionately cited. Two trends, both aimed at rigour and transparency, threaten to narrow how this research is done. We asked what these trends are, and how to meet their aims without their costs.

What we did. In this handbook chapter, we drew on recent debates in management and marketing about methodological templates and about the replication crisis in experimental psychology.

What we found. The first trend is template creep: growing pressure to follow standard formulas for collecting and analyzing data. The second is the push to make qualitative studies replicable by preregistering them and sharing their data. We propose guidelines for researchers, reviewers, editors, mentors and journals that keep rigour while leaving room for varied, creative research.
